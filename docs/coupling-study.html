TB-Port Coupling Study

Five ways to build the interface every TidyBot module attaches through, drawn to one scale and judged against what the robot actually has to survive.

The port has four jobs: capture a module arriving misaligned, locate it to under 0.1 mm, retain it against gravity and driving loads, and connect 24 V and CAN. Every option below does all four. They differ in where on the flange those jobs happen — and that sets how much of the robot's height the connector eats.

CriterionWhat it means here
Mates every time500 cycles unattended. No jamming, no partial seats, no retries.
Low profileMeasured as volume of each section consumed, not depth alone.
Power and data through24 V and CAN from the middle section out to both neighbours.
Survives dust and waterVacuuming, mopping, hair, grit. The joint cannot be a fair-weather part.
Holds under lift192 N in tension when the 3-stack flies — see section 04.

The governing insight: everything expensive in the original design traces to concentrating all four jobs into a small central boss. At small radius, tilt resistance and capture both have to be bought with depth. Spread the same jobs across the full Ø90 flange and they get cheap in exactly the dimension the base is short of. Radius is free; depth is charged twice, once per port.

D is the outlier worth dwelling on. Every other option needs something to push the halves together; D gets that for free because the robot is already driving into the dock. The cost is that it locates exactly in only two axes — the third depends on an end stop, so you would add a kinematic stop at the end of travel — and it demands a straight-line approach, which constrains dock geometry more than an axial drop does.

E in plan — and why it changes the metric

E is deeper than B on paper, and that is misleading. Depth only matters where material is actually removed, and E removes it at three corner bores rather than across a Ø90 disc. It consumes 8,143 mm³ of the base against the bayonet's 69,979 mm³ — and leaves the entire centre untouched.

The posts cannot be conductors — correcting an earlier claim

An earlier draft of this page said the dowel could be an outer conductor and the pin an inner one, giving two isolated circuits per post. That is wrong, and it is worth being precise about why. It describes a coaxial pin running down the post's axis. The actual mechanism is not that: the pin runs perpendicular, through a cross-hole, and bears against the inside of that hole. That contact is the load path. Metal pressed against metal under 64 N is a single electrical node, not two.

An insulating bushing between them would be structurally fine: 64 N over a 5 × 5 mm bearing is 2.6 MPa, a 47× margin against PEEK. But it puts a wear surface in the load path of a wet, gritty joint, and the failure mode is two circuits shorting — one of them the 24 V bus.

And the wet environment settles it anyway. Posts are exposed whenever a module is off, and this robot mops. Two energised posts plus a film of detergent water is a bridged supply, in the wettest place on the machine. Steel is electrically excellent — a Ø5 pin is 0.21 mΩ, a 1.7 mV drop at 8 A — but that was never the constraint.

So: posts carry load only. All eight contacts go in the sealed central connector, behind the perimeter gasket, in the space E frees up. That also disposes of the CAN problem — H and L on posts 160 mm apart would have been a loop antenna beside three motors, precisely what ADR 0003 chose CAN to avoid. In the centre they stay a proper twisted pair.

03

Designing for dust and water

This robot vacuums, mops and dusts. The coupling lives in fine abrasive dust, detergent water, hair and grit — and it has to mate reliably 500 times in that. Treating this as a first-class constraint rather than an afterthought changes the ranking.

The useful realisation is that contamination tolerance is mostly orthogonal to the coupling mechanism. It comes down to five rules that can be applied to almost any of the options — except where a mechanism structurally violates one.

Post-and-pin is the only option whose mechanism never sees the room. The posts are solid steel with nothing to jam; the pins, cam plate and actuator are all inside the base's sealed volume. B is the opposite case — a rotating collar with ramped slots, mounted on the outside of a robot that generates abrasive dust for a living.

Three posts, not four

A rigid body has six degrees of freedom, and each post in a socket removes two. Three posts is exactly determinate; four is over-constrained by two. In printed plastic that difference is the whole ballgame — with four, one post lands first and the other three fight it, so the joint either rocks or jams. Three tapered posts seat cleanly every time and can rotate a fraction to self-centre, which also softens the yaw sensitivity that was the concept's main weakness.

The taper earns its place twice: a 15 mm tip at 20° gives 5.5 mm of capture per post, and because it lives on the post rather than in the base, that capture costs zero receptacle depth. It is the only option here where capture is free.

The contactless variant

Worth keeping on the table: inductive power and optical data, which removes exposed electrical contacts altogether — the standard answer in washdown industrial equipment. At the top port's 40 W it is easy, costing about 4 W of coil heat. At the bottom port's 150 W it means roughly 18 W to shed inside the joint, which is real but not disqualifying. Pancake coils are 2–3 mm thick, so it costs no height, and an IR emitter and photodiode for data are trivial and completely immune to water.

This is orthogonal to the mechanical choice — it can be bolted onto any of the options. The honest cost is efficiency, complexity and money in every module, which is the thing the project has been carefully keeping cheap.

04

Making it self-aligning

Yaw sensitivity is the one real weakness of a multi-post joint, and it is worth attacking directly rather than tolerating. The fix is not a better taper — it is refusing to let one feature do two jobs.

Decompose the error

A feature at the centre sits at r = 0, so yaw does not displace it at all — it can kill lateral error while being completely blind to rotation. A feature at radius r sees r·θ from yaw — so it is the right thing to kill rotation, once lateral error is already gone.

Ask the posts to absorb both at once and they need 14.2 mm of capture against a taper that provides 5.5. Split the job and they need 0.74 mm — a 7.4× margin on the same taper. The geometry does not change; the sequencing does.

Conveniently, the central connector needs a protective shroud anyway. Make that shroud a tapered cone that engages before the posts and the primary alignment feature costs nothing extra — it is a part the design already had to build.

Put the compliance in the dock

The highest-leverage change is not on the robot at all. If the module sits in a compliant cradle in the dock — sprung, or on a floating mount — then the tapers generate a centring force and the cradle simply lets the module move to meet the robot. This is the remote-centre-compliance trick from robotic assembly, and it costs nothing on the robot or on any module. You build it once, in the dock.

Move the posts inboard

Yaw offset scales directly with post radius, and the structural penalty for coming inboard is negligible:

Post radiusYaw offset at 2°Moment load per post pair
r = 80 mm2.79 mm20 N
r = 65 mm2.27 mm25 N

Halving the radius halves the yaw problem and doubles a load that was already trivial against a 47× bearing margin. r = 50 is close to free.

One idea that does not work

Staggering the post lengths so they engage in sequence sounds like it should help — a first post lands, then the rest follow. It makes things worse. Seating one post first moves the centre of rotation off-axis onto that post, so the second post now sees r·√3·θ instead of r·θ — a 73% harder job.

Which is the same lesson from the other direction: the first feature to engage has to be the central one. Anything else establishes a pivot in the wrong place.

05

Forcing one orientation

Three posts at 120° have three-fold symmetry, so a module can mate three ways. Only one of them is right — and this matters even though the posts no longer carry current.

Two reasons it still matters. The central connector has eight contacts that must land on their opposite numbers. And more fundamentally, modules themselves have a front: a vacuum's brush roll and suction throat face the direction of travel, a dustbin has a door, a rotor unit has a forward axis. Orientation has to be deterministic regardless of how power gets across.

Unequal spacing, not unequal posts

The instinct to shape the posts differently works, but it is the expensive version. Simply move one post off the symmetric 120° and the wrong orientations become physically impossible — no new features, no new parts, no extra height.

Why not different diameters

Sizing the posts Ø10 / Ø8 / Ø6 also works, and all three are enormously strong enough — the smallest still carries 11 kN in double shear against a 64 N requirement. The objection is not strength, it is that the smallest post now sets the joint's rating, and you have three different parts to make, stock, inspect and replace instead of one.

Unequal spacing keeps every post identical. That matters more than it sounds: one dowel size, one socket size, one lip seal, one reamer, one spare. The asymmetry lives in the plate the posts are mounted in, which is printed anyway and costs nothing to make asymmetric.

A useful side effect: because the misfit is angular, it grows with radius. At r = 50 a 5° error is 4.4 mm, far beyond any taper's capture range, so a misaligned module cannot even start to enter — it sits proud, visibly and detectably, rather than jamming halfway.

06

Lift inverts the top port

The top module is meant to gain lift — the rotor unit that carries the robot over stairs. That reverses the sign of the force at the top port, and it is the single most consequential load case in the whole project. It disqualifies one of the options above.

Parked, the top port is in compression: a module sits on the base and gravity holds it there. Under lift, the entire robot — base plus whatever is hanging off the bottom — is suspended from that same joint in tension, with thrust and shock multiplying it.

This eliminates Option C for the top port. The three-ball coupling is stiff in compression because the balls take the moment out at r = 35. Put the joint in tension and the balls simply lift off their vees. All that remains is the single central pin at r = 0 — which is a pivot. Moment stiffness collapses to the bending stiffness of one pin, and the joint becomes a hinge holding a swinging 5 kg robot. That is the worst possible failure mode for something under a rotor.

Option B survives well: its lugs work in shear at r = 40, which is direction-agnostic, and a worm-driven collar cannot back-drive, so nothing releases in flight. Option A's ball detent is the classic cam-out geometry and I would not trust it in tension. Option D's rails are strong perpendicular to the slide, but a spring latch is the only thing standing between 19.6 kg and a dropped robot.

And because the stack flies as three — the tool stays attached — the bottom port is in tension too. Only 59 N of it, comfortably inside the existing 8 kg rating, but tension is a question of direction, not magnitude. C's balls lift off at 59 N exactly as they do at 192 N.

That kills the idea of splitting the spec. Both ports face the same kind of load, so they want the same mechanism — and one spec means any module still fits either port. C survives only if its central latch preloads harder than the flight tension (a joint stays stiff only while preload exceeds applied load), which means >192 N through a single pin bearing on printed plastic. Option B needs no such trick: its lugs work in shear and do not care which way the load points.

The base is now a structural member

This is the consequence that reaches furthest outside the port. In flight the load path runs lift module → top port → base structure → bottom port → tool. The base shell was designed to house components; it now has to carry 192 N straight through its middle. The two ports have to be structurally tied — ideally coaxial, with a column between them — and that column lands exactly where the Ø20 harness bore already is. It wants to be a tube.

One more reason stiffness matters more than it did an hour ago: rigid ports keep this a rigid-body control problem. A stiff stack is a single body with a low centre of mass, which is conventional to fly. A compliant one is a slung load under a multirotor, which is a materially harder control problem. The coupling is what keeps the vehicle on the easy side of that line.

07

Why A is out

Three bugs, all in the same congested ring between the bore wall and the flange edge — only 19.8 mm wide on the current spec. Each is fixable in an afternoon. Together they are evidence that the geometry is over-packed, which is a statement about the architecture rather than about any one dimension.

1

Coupon plate back-filled the ball sockets

Fusing the test plate onto a finished port unioned solid material into all three sockets: 264 mm³ of void in the port, 0.0 mm³ in the printed part.

2

Vee dowels collide with the boss

Channels run from r = 18 to 52 mm against a bore wall at r = 25.2, so a real Ø3 dowel intersects the seated boss by 19.4 mm³ — and nothing is left to support its inner end.

3

The kinematic coupling never engages

A sign error puts the dowels 4.0 mm too deep. The balls float 3.45 mm short and the plastic flanges collide first — the exact failure the coupling exists to prevent.

09

Where I land

Three tapered posts with cross pins. Power through the posts, signals down the middle.

Against the five criteria it is not close. Mates every time: three posts is exactly determinate — six constraints for six degrees of freedom — where four is over-constrained and will rock or jam in printed plastic. The tapers self-centre. Low profile: 8,143 mm³ of base consumed against 64–70k for everything else, and the capture taper rides on the post, so it costs zero receptacle depth. Holds under lift: pins in double shear, 164× margin, indifferent to load direction.

And it is the only option whose mechanism never sees the room. The pins, cam plate and motor are sealed inside the base; what is exposed is solid steel posts and drained holes, with nothing that can foul. B is the mirror image — a ramped rotating collar on the outside of a machine that manufactures abrasive dust for a living.

Posts carry load only; all electricals go in the sealed centre. The pin must bear on the post to carry the load, which makes them one electrical node — so three posts give three circuits, not the eight the spec needs. And energised posts on a mopping robot are a bridged bus waiting for a puddle. E frees the entire centre of the port, so the full contact block lives there, dry behind the perimeter gasket, with CAN as a proper twisted pair. Orientation is forced by putting one post at 235° rather than 240° — see section 05.

Yaw sensitivity is solved by staging, not by tolerance. Asking the posts to absorb lateral and angular error together needs 14.2 mm of capture against a 5.5 mm taper — it fails. Let a central cone kill lateral first (it sits at r = 0, so yaw cannot displace it) and the posts need 0.74 mm, a 7.4× margin on the same taper. Move the posts to r = 50 and the margin grows again, for a moment load that is still trivial. See section 04.

What it still costs. The sockets on the base's top face point upward and will collect dust; they need lip seals and a drain out the side wall, not into the electronics. And two of the five alignment stages live in the docking station, which is barely designed — so the dock is now on the critical path rather than a later concern. B remains the fallback if docking accuracy disappoints badly.

What has to be settled either way

  1. What does the lift module weigh? REQUIREMENTS.md has no line for it. At an estimated 2 kg the all-up mass is 6.9 kg, which needs about 13.8 kg of thrust at 2:1. Every load number on this page moves with that estimate.
  2. Do the two ports go coaxial? The flight load path effectively requires it, and it changes the base's internal layout — battery and compute have to move out of the centre column.
  3. How accurately does the robot dock? Sets the capture requirement, and it is still a guess. A Phase 4 measurement.
  4. What is the shock factor on landing? The 2× assumed here is a placeholder, and the top port's rating is directly proportional to it.
